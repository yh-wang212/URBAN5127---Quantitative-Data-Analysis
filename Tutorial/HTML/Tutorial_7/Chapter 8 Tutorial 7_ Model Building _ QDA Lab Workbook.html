8.1 Introduction



From Lab Tutorial 5, my research question is:


“The price of an Airbnb listing can be predicted primarily by the type
of room (room_type), even when controlling for other characteristics
like number of rooms, review scores and even property type.”


So I have a dependent variable (price), and a key independent variable
that I’m primarily interested in (room_type) and control variables
that will help isolate the unique effect of my variable of interest.


We see this approach in the lecture example: the unique effect of Gender
on pay was isolated by adding in groups of control variables (Additional
Demographics, Job characteristics, Firm characteristics etc.).


Let’s create a model building strategy. My first model is my
Baseline Model: My Research Question (DV-Key IV) and so I will need
my two variables price and room_type.


What are my Rival or Alternative explanations? OK, I’ll need to make
this up for pedagogical purposes since I don’t know the literature or
the area well. You will need to do this remember though.


OK, I’m thinking the following:


Rival or Alternative explanation 1: Property characteristics. I need
to control not just for room type but for other characteristics of an
Airbnb letting: not just whether it has a private room but whether it is
a private room in an expensive hotel or in a boat or in a windmill. So I
am wanting to control for all other property type characteristics. I
only have one variable here to choose from: property_type. I haven’t
chosen this dataset myself so I’ll just need to go with this. This will
also explain price in addition to my Research Question (room_type)


Rival or Alternative explanation 2: Hedonic influences. I am vaguely
aware of the literature on hedonic pricing so (and I am drastically
simplifying!), another room is going to add value. Obviously, a room
with 8 bedrooms is going to be more expensive than a room with only
1: and that’s regardless of whether it is a hotel or windmill or
whatever. So this is an additional factor which needs to be accounted
for, as it surely explains price also, in addition to what my Research
Question puts forward.


Rival or Alternative explanation 3: Quality. Now surely if a listing
gets really good reviews, it’ll be more expensive? Higher quality (which
reviews measure) will probably mean higher prices, right? OK, we’re
going with this for now but we’ve already seen this assumption is
problematic (when the correlation was negative not positive as we
expected). Let’s pretend we don’t know this (never do this) and proceed.
What do variables do we need to measure our Quality rival explanation:
we have review_scores_rating so we’ll use that.


This is just a quick cobbling together for pedagogical purposes. Make
sure you take time here however as this is very important. You need to
demonstrate a well thought out modelling strategy if you are going to
have a well thought out answer to your Research Questione: the two are
not separate.


So putting this all together: we have our Baseline Model (Model 1: Our
Research Question) and then we have 3 Rival or Alternative
explanations:


    

  1. Research Question: My baseline bivariate model (room_type)
    2. 

  2. Property characteristics: Add in the other Type variable
(property_type)
    3. 

  3. Hedonic Influences: Add in the Rooms variables (bathrooms,
bedrooms, accommodates)
    4. 

  4. Quality: Add in Reviews (review_scores_rating)
    5. 



So I know how many models to build now: My Baseline and however many
Rival or Alternative explanations I need to account for (here 3).







8.2 Model 1: Basic bivariate model



I will begin with my baseline model therefore, containing my
dependent variable and independent variable of interest only: the simple
bivariate model which represents the bivariate relationship of my
Research Question (DV-Key IV). I can then subsequently control for the
other important explanatory factors of price in subsequent models
(Models 2-4), using a Specific to General approach to model building.


But let’s build model 1:

We need to interpret our model now :


Basic interpretation (Bivariate): For every one-unit increase in x,
y changes by [the coefficient] on average.


This is straightforward for numeric variables but for factor variables,
we need to realise that a one unit change is not along a continuous
numeric scale. Instead it is a change in the category e.g. from 0
(reference category: Male) to 1 (Female). So what we are doing with
factor variables is comparing each category (or factor level) to a
reference category. This reference category will not appear as a
coefficient: because it is the thing we are comparing to.


In our simple example above: we compared Female to the reference
category (Male). We only get one coefficient, therefore (Female): which
quantifies the difference to Males.


So the first thing we need to do to interpret a factor variable then is
understand the reference category. Note that R will take the very first
level (or category) of the factor variable and make that the reference
category by default. Let’s look at the levels of the factor variable
room_type to check what this will be:


levels(listings$room_type)


## [1] "Entire home/apt" "Private room"    "Shared room"


The very first level is Entire home/apt so this is our reference
category.


We can now proceed to interpretation:


    

  • A private room listing is going to be $81.35 less (-81.348) on
average than a listing which is an entire home or apartment (the
reference category).
    • 

  • A shared room listing is going to be $100.76 more (100.766) on
average than an entire home or apartment listing.
    • 



We need to use our common sense and specific knowledge (e.g. our
research area of interest) when we look at regression coefficients. Here
common sense tells us that a shared room is not going to be more
expensive than having a whole apartment or home to yourself. So
something is not right here and, from the tutorial, we saw that our
prior work in previous tutorials told us that shared rooms only had 69
observations and was distributed quite differently to the other two
levels of room_type.


In this instance, we can interpret the intercept. The intercept can be
defined as where the regression line crosses the y axis. If you draw
this out as a graph or plot, you will see that this is, at the same
time, where x = 0.


Here x (or our independent variable ) = 0 is our reference category:
Entire home/apt . So the coefficient here (142.582) means that, on
average (i.e. not for every entire home or apartment in the dataset),
an entire home or apartment listing will cost $142.58.


Sometimes the intercept will not make any sense, however, especially
with numeric variables (e.g. Age = 0).





8.2.1 Model 1: Baseline is our inferential test



Notice that the t-test is the same as our Baseline Model.


# 3. Is the difference in price statistically significant?
listings_subset <- filter(listings, room_type != "Shared room") 

t.test(price ~ room_type, data = listings_subset)


## 
##  Welch Two Sample t-test
## 
## data:  price by room_type
## t = 40.419, df = 13419, p-value < 2.2e-16
## alternative hypothesis: true difference in means between group Entire home/apt and group Private room is not equal to 0
## 95 percent confidence interval:
##  77.40350 85.29348
## sample estimates:
## mean in group Entire home/apt    mean in group Private room 
##                     142.58211                      61.23363


When we look at the output from the t-test:


    

  • the mean for Entire home/appt is 142.58211: this is the same as
the intercept in our baseline regression model.
    • 

  • 

    the mean for Private Room is 61.23363: our coefficient is
-81.348, the difference between Entire home/appt and Private Room.
So how much does a Private Room actually cost on average> Let’s
work it out:
    

    142.58211 (intercept = Entire home/appt) - 81.34848 (Private Room
coefficient) = 61.23363
    

    • 



It costs $61.23. The same as the mean price for Private Room.


So the t-test gives us:


    

  1. the mean cost for Entire home/appt ($142.58)
    2. 

  2. and the mean cost for Private Room ($61.23).
    3. 



The regression model gives us


    

  1. the mean cost for Entire home/appt (intercept)
    2. 

  2. and the difference between Entire home/appt and Private Room
(the coefficient). That is, instead of the mean for Private Room
the only difference is it gives us the difference between Entire
home/appt and Private Room.
    3. 



The only difference between the t-test and the baseline is number two
highlighted above. The coefficient remember tells us the associated
change in the price. In a factor variable this is the difference
between the reference category (Entire home/appt) and the other category
(or categories).


In other words, the t-test and the baseline model are the same: it is
the same thing (t-test, baseline model) expressed in a slightly
different way. This is always the case: your inferential test (t-test,
ANOVA, correlation) is your Baseline Model. This was also what was
meant (mainly) in the lecture: that a t-test or ANOVA is just a special
case of linear regression.

By adding in a second variable, the model moves from being a simple
bivariate model to a multiple linear regression model. Which means we
see the unique effect of our variable of interest room_type, because
we are now controlling for property_type.


We can now add this element to our template for interpretation:


    

  1. Basic interpretation (Bivariate): For every one-unit increase in
x, y changes by [the coefficient] on average.
    2. 

  2. Controlling for (Multiple): Regardless of the other independent
variable(s) (or controlling for them).
    3. 



This means we can isolate the unique effect of our independent variable
of interest.


Interpretation: Using the template above, we can then say that:


    

  1. 
Controlling for (Multiple): regardless of whether the listing
is a windmill or boutique hotel, a boat or an apartment (i.e.
controlling for property_type)…
    2. 



    

  1. 
Basic interpretation: … a Private Room is going to cost
$89.65 less (-89.650) than an entire home or apartment (the
reference category) on average (i.e. not for every single individual
observation of Private Room in the dataset).
    2. 



Having done so, we want to compare Model 1 and Model 2.


Firstly, we want to compare:


Coefficients:


    

  • The statistical significance of the coefficients (p-values)
    • 

  • The sign of the coefficients (negative or positive)
    • 

  • The magnitude of the coefficients (the actual number)
    • 

  • Are the coefficient values/signs reasonable?
    • 



Explanatory Power:


    

  • The R2 and adjusted R2 value.
    • 



Improved model fit (less error or improved predictive power):


    

  • The standard error (see Fogarty reading): is it going down?
    • 



Dropped Observations:


    

  • Were any observations deleted due to missingness?
    • 



And then secondly, we want to understand what this model (compared
to the previous) has added or accomplished i.e. what have we controlled
for?


Specifically, we want to understand if our independent variable of
interest has changed: have the coefficients gone up/down or changed
sign? We said that a simple bivariate regression model will most likely
have omitted variable bias: as we conceptually control for other likely
factors (Types/Rooms/Reviews as here or Demographics/Jobs/Firms/Regions
as in Assignment 2), then we increasingly isolate the unique effect of
our variable of interest. In doing so, the coefficient is likely to
change. What did we control for then and how is that related to
change in the coefficient?


We want to do this here as well. The Airbnb dataset is a bit messy and
we have not chosen our control variables ourselves (the variables have
been chosen for us) so this is more difficult. I give an example from
Assignment 2, therefore: after building Model 3 and controlling for
Job Characteristics, the Gender coefficient (the variable of interest)
changes from -22% to -11% (it’s a percentage change because the
dependent variable has been log transformed). Here is the explanation
for the reduction:


In Model 3 we introduce characteristics of the job. Part-time and
non-permanent positions seem to have lower hourly wages. There is no
statistically significant association with wages. As expected, more
senior positions tend to have higher wages. We also note that in this
model, the estimated wage gap is smaller. This is probably due to the
fact that women are more likely to occupy part-time positions (which
have lower wages) and they are less likely to occupy the most senior
positions. (Assignment 2 example)


You would want to take a similar approach. It would also be useful to
interpret the individual control coefficients added (i.e. rather than
just interpreting the whole chunk/group of variables that you added to
the new model).





8.3.0.1 The intercept



Note now, at this point, the intercept has changed meaning: x = 0 now
means that it is the average price for both reference categories: for
an Entire home/apt (room_type reference category) and an
Aparthotel (property_type reference category). I’m not even sure
that makes substantive sense. But, in other words, it represents x = 0
for every new independent variable we enter into our model.


By the time of our final model, we will have entered in six independent
variables: room_type, property_type, bathrooms, bedrooms,
accommodates and review-scores_rating. The intercept will represent
where x = 0 for all of them i.e. or an Entire home/apt (room_type
reference category) and an Aparthotel (property_type reference
category) with zero bathrooms, zero bedrooms, zero accommodates and a
zero review score. So again, you can see even more clearly that the
intercept often does not make any substantive sense and is more of a
mathematically necessary part of generating the linear model.

8.6 Summary of our models



We created a model building strategy so we knew what we were were
controlling for: that is, our Rival our Alternative Explanations. And
therefore how many models to build:


    

  1. Research Question: My baseline bivariate model (room_type)
    2. 

  2. Property characteristics: Add in the other Type variable
(property_type)
    3. 

  3. Hedonic Influences: Add in the Rooms variables (bathrooms,
bedrooms, accommodates)
    4. 

  4. Quality: Add in Reviews (review_scores_rating)
    5. 



Each time, we were not adding in more control variables randomly, but
achieving something conceptually and substantively by controlling for
another concept (Property characteristics/Hedonic
Influences/Quality or, as in the course example,
Demographics/Jobs/Industry/Regions).


We then had an interpretation strategy which both helped us
interpret our models but also how they related to our Research
Question: since this is the whole point of building a model in the
first place:


    

  • Step 1: Interpret the Model (within the model)
using the key questions from the lecture (i.e. coefficients,
p-values, R squared etc.)
    •

8.7 Final Summary



Once we have our final model and after we have tested it (Tutorials
9-10), we would return to our research question and use it to answer
our question:


“The price of an Airbnb listing can be predicted primarily by the type
of room (room_type), even when controlling for other characteristics
like number of rooms, review scores and even property type.”


I would probably need to qualify this answer by saying that Room Type
alone was a good predictor of price but not sufficient in itself: Rooms
(bathrooms, bedrooms etc.) is also necessary. As discussed in the
Tutorial, ideally this process would proceed with a substantive research
question (and derived hypotheses) informed by prior literature. We’ve
tried to simulate this process as best as we could, however.


In doing so, the main elements you should take away are:


    

  1. The difference between simple linear regression (bivariate) and
multiple linear regression (multivariable). The value of multiple
linear regression is statistically it ‘partials out’ the influence
of other variables on our dependent variable. We can then isolate
the unique effect of our variable of interest (our key independent
variable)
    2. 

  2. How our inferential test is our simple linear regression
    3. 

  3. Why we need to proceed from our inferential test/simple linear
regression onto multiple linear regression by adding control
variables.
    4. 

  4. A model building strategy: thinking through what conceptually we
need to control for
    5. 

  5. An interpretation strategy: interpreting each additional model in
itself but also in relation to our Research Question - in other
words, the DV-IV pair or inferential test/simple linear
regression we started off with i.e. our baseline model. We can track
the ‘story’ of this as it progresses through each subsequent model.
By the time we reach our final model, we have a much fuller story
with which to answer our Research Question.
    6.
